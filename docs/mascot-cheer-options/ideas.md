# The mascot as a supporter: the idea list

10 November 2026, Queensland. The owner asked for ideas "like how Duolingo does it or other fancy apps" for the mascot to cheer finished work across the whole book, motivate the team to get prep done and use the book more, maybe with speech bubbles. This is the brainstorm. The designs, the council and the recommendation are in [the brief](brief.md).

How it was made: four readers mapped every "job done" moment in the code (80 moments on Prep, Orders, Wastage, Transfers, Deliveries, Cleaning, cooling, functions, minutes, mail, guides and Home). Four researchers read how Duolingo and other apps do it, the evidence on rewards at work, and speech bubble craft (78 findings, most read at the source, each marked fetched, search result or from memory). Four independent lenses then wrote 46 ideas: a Duolingo designer and copywriter, a line cook turned head chef, a calm habit designer, and a delight designer paired with a skeptic. The lead merged them into the 30 below. This is desk work by role, not research with the kitchen's staff.

## What the research says, in six lines

1. **Duolingo's wins are small cheers at real moments, not a mascot everywhere.** A short animation when a streak is extended lifted new learners' 7 day return by 1.7%. Wording and a cap on how often ("protect the channel") mattered as much as the art.
2. **The loved apps cheer finished, confirmed work, now and then, at the edges of the work.** Mailchimp's high five comes after the send. Asana's creatures "don't appear every time". Every one of them can be turned down.
3. **What helps at work: seeing real progress and the finish line, shared team goals, and specific thanks nobody expected.** Surprise thank-you cards lifted output about 7% in one study.
4. **What backfires: ranks, points, streaks, and any reward for speed.** Taking a rank display away raised sales 11% in one study. A regulator told a warehouse firm to remove pace incentives. Time pressure is a known food safety risk.
5. **Praise that informs helps. Praise that rates the person backfires.** "Larder is done. Grill has 2 left." beats "Great job, Ann!".
6. **Novelty fades in about four weeks.** Few triggers, tied to real finish lines, with the words rotated.

Sources and the full findings are with the session's working files, summarised in [the brief](brief.md#research).

## The rules every idea follows

- **Only real, confirmed work.** He speaks after the server has the tick. Never on a line that is Not sent, never on a refused save. Undo takes the cheer back.
- **Say what was finished and what is next.** The section and the count. Never who is good.
- **Never the clock.** No fast, early, quick, record or beat. The book does not even store how long a line took: the tick clears the On it time.
- **The section and the kitchen, not the person.** Above all on the shared tablet.
- **Rare beats constant.** An ordinary tick keeps today's plain slip. A cook ticks 5 to 30 lines a day: a cheer on each is noise by the tenth.
- **Never in the way.** No pop-up, nothing over a list line, no moved tap target, no sound. One clip at a time, played once, then still. Reduced motion keeps the words.
- **Two moods only: resting and pleased.** No sad mascot, no nagging, no guilt.
- **Quiet zones.** Cooling records, temperatures, recipe methods, the PIN and Who are you? screens, red failures, Not sent and No signal. A face there would look like a second status or a blessing on food safety.
- **A way out from day one.** Each device: Cheers, Words only, Off.

## Left out, and why

- **A cheer on every tick.** Noise by the tenth, hated by the fortieth, and it blurs the slip that carries Undo.
- **Personal streaks, points, levels, "You ticked 14 lines".** A day off breaks them, they tell staff the book is counting them, and they reward ticks, not prep.
- **Praise for speed.** The honest way to faster is to show how little is left.
- **Sound and vibration.** A shared tablet would chirp for everyone, and iPhones do not vibrate from a web page.
- **A floating bubble, a sheet that opens by itself, a mascot button in the top bar, a loop beside a list.** Each breaks a service rule.
- **A named cheer on the shared tablet.** Name the section. Personal thanks go to a personal phone or come from the chef in person.
- **Random rewards.** Every trigger is one a cook can explain.

## What each clip means

So no clip is ever mistaken for a status. From the copywriter's lens; the head chef's choice of 9 November (all 11 in the morning shuffle) stands unless changed.

| Clip | Use for | Never for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idle | Rest. "Nothing to say" | Loading, saving, waiting |
| Welcome | Hello and good night, to a person | PIN and Who are you? screens |
| Look around | A true empty page | Loading, no signal, "you forgot" |
| Recipe reading | Minutes signed, a guide finished | Recipe cards and methods |
| Ready to cook | The start: "You are on Larder today" | Beside a cut-off or a count |
| Smash | A hard line is gone | Speed, every tick, a how-to |
| Flip | One section done, more to go | Undo or refresh |
| Build | Parts put together: orders placed | Recipe build drawings |
| Serve | The big finish only: "Burger's up" | Saved, Sent, any single tick |
| Walk | Carrying between people: thanks, deliveries | Sending or loading |
| At the smoker | The slow close of the day | Cooling, batches, temperatures |

## How the head chef would know it works

Without counting people:

- **The lists themselves.** More days the list is cleared, fewer lines rolling over, tomorrow's list written the night before more often. Read beside bookings.
- **Records kept, for the whole kitchen.** Days with a wastage entry, nights cleaning reached all done.
- **Ask the team.** Three questions in the minutes at week 4 and week 10: is it useful, is it in the way, have you turned it down?
- **Harm checks.** More ticks undone soon after ticking, junk entries, ticks bunched at close. If any rise, pull that cheer back first.
